Building mini-soc: a local SOC with osquery, Kafka, ML, and an LLM analyst

June 2026 · 9 min read · github.com/a-yousafzai/mini-soc

I spend my day job running SIEM and endpoint security at the scale of multiple terabytes per day across 1.4M endpoints. There's a thing about that scale: most days you don't actually see the system. You see the dashboards, the SLOs, the budget meetings, the incident retros. The pipes themselves are abstractions.

I missed seeing the pipes.

So I built mini-soc — a self-contained Security Operations Center that runs on my laptop. osquery on the endpoint, Kafka in the middle, Elasticsearch + Kibana on the back, two ML services consuming the topics, and an LLM-backed AI analyst summarizing what comes out the other side. It's the same architectural shape as a real SOC, compressed into docker compose up.

This post is about why I built it, what's in it, and what it taught me about building AI-powered security products — which is what I actually want to spend the next decade doing.

Why a "mini" SOC, not a real one

Three reasons.

One: I wanted a sandbox where I could break things. In a 24/7 enterprise SIEM, you don't get to swap out the message bus on a Tuesday afternoon to see what happens. In mini-soc, I can.

Two: I wanted a place to test ideas about LLMs in detection & response. The honest version of "AI in security" right now is: vendors over-promise, practitioners under-trust, and nobody quite knows what the right unit of work is for an LLM in a SOC. The only way to develop a real opinion is to ship something — even a small thing — and watch it behave.

Three: I wanted my hands on osquery again. I was the team lead for open-source endpoint visibility at Comcast for years. osquery was a daily companion. mini-soc is partly nostalgia.

The architecture

The shape mirrors what most enterprise SOCs actually look like, just collapsed onto one host.

endpoint            transport            storage / UI            intelligence
─────────           ──────────            ─────────────           ─────────────
osqueryd  ──┐
            ├──► Logstash ──► Kafka ──► Elasticsearch ──► Kibana
syslog    ──┘                  │
                               ├──► ml-service          (syslog · Drain3)
                               ├──► ml-service-osquery  (anomaly scoring)
                               └──► ai-analyst          (LLM summarization)

Every piece is a deliberate choice. A few worth calling out.

osquery as the endpoint sensor

osquery is the closest open-source equivalent to a real EDR data source. It exposes the host as a SQL-queryable surface — processes, sockets, file integrity, scheduled tasks. For a SOC sandbox, it gets you 80% of "what is this endpoint doing right now" without writing a single C++ line.

One small wrinkle worth documenting, because it bit me and will bite anyone who tries this: the macOS osquery build commonly installed locally does not include the Kafka logger plugin. So mini-soc takes a small, deliberate detour: osquery writes to a filesystem log, Logstash tails it, Logstash publishes to Kafka. You still get the Kafka hop — you just don't have to rebuild osquery from source to get there. If you have a build with the plugin, the native path is supported too.

Kafka in the middle, even at toy scale

You don't need Kafka for a single laptop. You can stream osquery directly into Elasticsearch and call it a day. I put Kafka in anyway because the entire point of mini-soc is to mirror the shape of real systems, and in real systems, the message bus is the thing that changes everything.

Specifically: once you have a durable bus, you can fan out consumers without coupling them to ingestion. The two ML services and the AI analyst all consume the same topics independently. If one of them crashes, the bus doesn't care. If I want to add a fourth consumer tomorrow — say, a detection-rule engine — I don't have to touch ingestion. That property is the entire reason real SOCs run on Kafka, and it's worth feeling at small scale.

Two ML services, doing different jobs

The syslog ML service uses Drain3 — a streaming log-template miner. It clusters log lines into templates and emits anomalies when a new template appears or when a known template's frequency shifts. It's the kind of thing that's been quietly powering log-anomaly products for years and almost nobody talks about.

The osquery ML service does straightforward anomaly scoring on event distributions — process names, parent-child trees, network destinations. It's not magic. It doesn't have to be. Most "weird" things on a host are weird because they haven't happened before, and a frequency-based scorer catches a surprising amount of that.

ai-analyst — the LLM piece

This is the part that matters most to me, and the part I think about every day at work.

The ai-analyst service consumes from Kafka and produces short, human-readable summaries of what the ML services flagged. It does not decide what is malicious. It does not autonomously close tickets. It does not take action. It writes a paragraph that an analyst can read in 10 seconds instead of a JSON blob they have to read in 90.

That distinction is the whole thesis of how I think LLMs should plug into security workflows today.

What this taught me about AI-powered security

I want to be specific here, because "AI for security" is a phrase that's lost most of its meaning.

1. The right unit of work for an LLM in a SOC is the analyst's reading time

Every alert in a real SOC has a hidden cost: the seconds an analyst spends parsing it. A noisy alert that takes 30 seconds to dismiss costs 30 seconds × the number of times it fires × the number of analysts in the rotation. Multiply that out across a year and you're looking at full FTEs burned on dismissal.

LLMs are extraordinary at compressing analyst reading time. A two-paragraph natural-language summary of a 4KB JSON event is the cheapest, most reliable, highest-leverage AI feature in security right now. It's also the least exciting. Vendors don't lead with it because it doesn't sound impressive. It just works.

2. Autonomous action is mostly a trap

"AI agent that triages every alert and closes the false positives" is a great pitch deck and a terrible production system. The failure mode is silent. The blast radius is the entire SOC. You will not catch the regression in evals.

What works instead: LLMs as copilots that propose. The human stays in the loop. The LLM gets graded on whether its proposals turned out to be right. Over time, you can promote specific narrow proposals to autonomous — "auto-close alerts of type X under condition Y where the LLM has been >99% correct for 90 days." That promotion has to be earned, per-class, with telemetry.

This is the model ai-analyst uses. It summarizes. A human reads. Maybe later, specific summaries graduate to specific automated actions. Not today.

3. Evaluation is the actual hard problem

The most important thing I learned writing ai-analyst is that summarizing security events is easy. Knowing whether the summary was accurate is the hard problem. There's no ground truth. The "correct" summary is a fuzzy thing — it depends on what the on-call analyst needs to know, what's already in their context, and what they were planning to do next.

This is the thing I'd want to spend real time on at a place that takes evaluation seriously. Anthropic is one of the few companies in the world that takes it seriously enough.

What's next for mini-soc

The repo is a sandbox, not a product, and I want to keep it that way. But there are three things I want to add:

  • A tiny detection-rule engine consuming from Kafka, so I can A/B classical rules vs. ML-driven anomaly scoring on the same input stream.
  • Eval harness for the AI analyst. Even a toy one. Pre-graded summaries, structured comparison, drift detection. The point isn't to score well; it's to build the muscle of always evaluating.
  • A scenario generator — synthetic but realistic intrusion patterns that produce the kind of telemetry a real SOC would see, so the whole pipeline can be exercised end-to-end on demand.

None of that is in the repo today. It will be.

Why I'm posting this

Because the gap between "I lead a SIEM platform at TB/day" and "I write code that LLMs touch" is something I want to close in public.

I've spent ten years building security products that protect millions of endpoints. The next ten, I want to spend building security products that are trustworthy — where AI helps the people who do the hard work, doesn't replace their judgment, and earns autonomy slowly. mini-soc is the smallest version of that I could ship in a weekend. It's not the work. It's a signal that I'm doing the work.

The repo is here: github.com/a-yousafzai/mini-soc. Issues and PRs welcome. Reach out if you're building in this space — I'd love to compare notes.
